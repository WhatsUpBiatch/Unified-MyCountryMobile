A deeper look at the AI, chatbot and omnichannel side changed one of the findings below. There is a service on this platform that meters usage, applies an allowance, checks the balance, charges the wallet and refuses to run when the wallet is empty. It is the AI agent. It is complete, it is correct, and it is live.

That makes M5 narrower than it reads — and much easier to fix, because the pattern to copy already exists in our own code. See Part 6, and Part 7 for the three reference add-ons this report had not yet covered.

The one-line summary

The shop works — a customer can pick a plan, pay by card, and the account turns on. Everything after the first payment is where the platform is thin: nothing charges for what people actually use, no tax is ever collected, nobody is chased when a payment fails, and the nightly billing job runs on three machines at once against one database. Before taking real money at scale, fix items M1 to M5 in Part 5.

Part 1 — How the reference product bills

Their billing is built on five separate ideas. Ours currently has one and a half of them. Understanding the five is the whole map.

IdeaWhat it meansWhy it exists
1. LicencesYou buy seats up front. You pay for a seat whether or not anyone sits in it. You can only create as many users as you have licences. Seats bought mid-month are prorated to the day. Seats removed stay live until the end of the cycle you already paid for.It is the predictable, recurring part of the bill. It is also the hard limit on how many people you can add — the licence count is the enforcement.
2. Credits (a wallet)A pre-paid balance for the things you cannot predict: calling minutes, SMS segments, fax pages, AI conversations. Spending order is fixed: plan allowance first → the matching commitment → the general credit wallet. Auto-recharge tops the wallet up when it drops below a set floor.Usage is variable. A wallet means a customer can never run up a surprise debt, and the company never gives away service it has not been paid for.
3. Usage historyEvery single chargeable event is a row: who, when, which number, direction, duration, rate, billing increment, which wallet paid, and what it cost. Filterable by user, month, service, amount. Exportable to CSV or Excel.This is the evidence. When a customer disputes a bill, you show them the row. Without it, every dispute is a refund.
4. Taxes and feesTelecom tax is not sales tax. On top of the plan price they add: federal universal service fund, a relay-service fee, a compliance cost-recovery fee, a per-line E911 fee (about $0.99 per user per month), plus state and local surcharges, plus GST/HST in Canada — all driven by the company's billing address.These are legally mandated. Not collecting them does not make them go away; it makes them a liability the platform pays out of its own margin.
5. Account lifecycleCard verification before the first payment. Then, if a payment fails: on hold (email the admin, banner in the app, a spoken reminder at the start of calls — but lines stay live), then suspended (lines dead, callers hear why). Self-service cancellation for small month-to-month accounts. A written refund rule: 3 days, no exceptions.A failed card is usually an expired card, not a deadbeat. The graded hold gives an honest customer time to fix it, and gives the business a defensible point at which to stop giving away service.

Things they have that are worth knowing about but are not launch blockers

  • Licence pool — unused seats float between offices of the same company automatically, one pool per currency. Only relevant once a customer has several sites.
  • Unified credit pool — one shared wallet across offices; each office spends its own allowance first, then the shared pot.
  • Flex vs contracted licences — flex is self-service, list price, prorated to the day, cancel any time. Contracted is a signed term at a better rate. Two different products with two different rules.
  • Cost centres — pure reporting labels. Split an office, a user, or a licence across up to 10 codes; each split must total exactly 100%; precedence is user → licence → office. They explicitly change nothing about the invoice.
  • Billing change log — every licence added or removed, filterable by user and month, exportable.
  • Credits analytics — trends by date range, service, and credit sub-type (domestic, international, toll-free, contact-centre inbound, contact-centre outbound).

Part 4 — What is actually wrong, with the proof

M1 · Three servers run the nightly billing job against the same database

Proof. mcm-new and mcm-ucaas3 both have DB_HOST=ucass-mysql-do-user-11297133-0… and DB_NAME=mycountrymobile_db, and both logs say "Cron jobs registered on PM2 instance 0". The job's guard only skips when NODE_APP_INSTANCE is set and is not "0" — it stops a second copy on the same machine, and nothing else. The database then shows the result: at 00:10 every night the same company gets 3 or 4 identical billing rows, seconds apart.

2026-09-04  Capanicus   3 rows  00:10:01, 00:10:02, 00:10:04
2026-09-04  Testers4    3 rows  00:10:02, 00:10:04, 00:10:08
2026-09-02  Capanicus   4 rows  00:10:03 ×3, 00:10:04

I confirmed two of the three writers. The third writes to production but I could not name the machine — the two clone sites (695 and ucaas.in) both use their own local databases and have crons switched off, and the switch box runs no API. That unidentified third writer should be found before anything else, because it is a machine nobody is accounting for that has write access to the live billing tables.

Why it has not hurt yet: the plan-renewal charge carries a safety key built from company + plan + licences + amount + today's date, so the card processor collapses the duplicates into one charge. Auto-recharge (M2) carries no such key. That is the exposure.

M2 · Auto-recharge can charge a customer many times over

Four faults stack up in one routine:

  1. It loops over every user on the platform, not every company — but the balance it checks and tops up is the company's. Five people in one company with auto-recharge on means five top-ups.
  2. The five run in parallel, so they all read the same low balance before any of them has credited it.
  3. The payment is created with no safety key, so the card processor cannot collapse duplicates.
  4. The job runs every two minutes, and the balance only rises when the processor's callback arrives. If that callback is slow or lost, it charges again two minutes later. Then again.

The in-process "already running" guard does not help, because of M1 — three processes, three guards.

Why it has not hurt yet: only two auto-recharges have ever fired, both on 31 July, because almost nobody has the setting on. At launch, that changes.

M3 · Tax is calculated and then thrown away

The tax routine calls the payment provider's tax service correctly, and returns a full breakdown: sub-total, tax amount, tax percentage, total. But the field the charging code uses — total_cost — is set to plan price × licences, the figure before tax. The renewal job then charges exactly that.

So even on the day tax registration is completed, the tax would still not be charged. It is a one-line fix in the return value, and it must be made before registering, not after.

Separately: 0 of 156 bills carry any tax, which is consistent with there being no tax registration on the payment provider account yet. And there is no concept at all of the telecom-specific fees (E911 per line, universal service, relay, compliance) that the reference itemises on every bill. Those are legal obligations, not optional line items.

M4 · Nobody is ever told their payment failed

When the renewal job cannot charge, it writes a row and moves on:

2026-09-04 00:10:11  MCM          $2.00   "Renewal skipped - no card"
2026-09-04 00:10:10  Testcompany  $6.00   "Plan renewal failed: Failed to list payments: Keys…"
2026-09-04 00:10:08  Testers4     $27.99  "Renewal skipped - no card"
2026-09-04 00:10:04  Capanicus    $30.00  "Renewal skipped - no card"

No email. No banner. No reminder. The same four rows are written again the next night, and the night after — 78 of them so far. The customer's first and only signal is that on day 7 every single user is locked out at once, with no warning in between.

There is also a gap in the code's own logic: the "no card" branch does not apply the two-days-overdue rule that the "card declined" branch does. A company with no card simply drifts, still fully working, until the separate 7-day sweep catches it.

Note one thing in the reference that is easy to miss and is the right design: on hold, the lines stay live. A failed card is usually an expired card. Cutting service on day one costs you the customer; cutting on day seven with no warning costs you the customer and a support fight.

M5 · Nothing charges for what people actually use

This is the biggest hole and the easiest one to miss, because several pieces of it exist and look healthy.

  • Rate cards are real and loaded — 92,622 outbound call rates, 651 SMS rates, fax and MMS cards.
  • A per-call cost is written into each tenant's call history. In one tenant, 53 of 153 calls carry a cost; total, $1.47.
  • But charge — the figure meant to be the customer's — is often 0 while cost is not. On 3 September: 14 answered calls, 10 rated, every charge 0.00.
  • Nothing carries any of it into the ledger. There is no "Balance Deduct" row in the entire billing history. Every company's call_duration_used is 0.
  • Inbound cannot be rated at all: every plan points at rate card a1b005f6-…, which does not exist.
  • Number rental produces bills of $0.00 — 19 of them.
  • Nothing checks a balance before placing a call.

The plain version: a customer on the $2 plan can make unlimited international calls and be charged $2. The carrier still invoices us for every minute.

Correction, second pass: this is true of calling, messaging and fax — but not of the AI agent, which does all of it correctly. Part 6 explains, and it is good news: the thing M5 asks for has already been written once, in this codebase, by us.

M6 · Three cancellations have been ignored since July

Three companies asked to cancel. Their requests sit at status "pending" with billing dates of 29 July, 30 July and 28 August. The renewal job does contain the code to honour a cancellation — but it only looks at companies whose plan has already expired and which reach that branch, and these three never got there. Meanwhile two of them have been charged nothing and one is still marked active.

M7 · The plan prices are wrong in two different directions

The yearly Ultimate price is broken data. Monthly $2, quarterly $6, six-month $12 — and then yearly shows an original price of $24 with a "25% discount" price of $378. The discounted price is fifteen times the original. Anyone choosing yearly to save money pays 15× more.

The screen and the database disagree. The plan catalogue in the website says Ultimate is $42 per seat per month. The database says $2, and $2 is what is charged — 14 companies are on it. One of those two numbers is a promise you are breaking; only you can say which.

M8 · Money endpoints trust the browser

  • /api/payment/charge (plan upgrade) takes company_uuid from the request body and never checks it against the signed-in user. The trial-upgrade endpoint does the same. The renewal endpoint does check — so the fix is a two-line copy from one file to another.
  • The same two endpoints take the licence count from the browser, and the licence count is what sets the price.
  • In four places a saved card is looked up by its id alone, with no company filter — Card.findOne({ where: { uuid: card_id } }). The card processor's own "this card belongs to another customer" check blocks the worst outcome, but the platform should never have needed rescuing by it.

This is exactly why the add-ons screen has no buy button today. That was the right decision; keep it until this is fixed.

M9 · Licence counts are sold but never enforced

Capanicus has 2,001 licences and 4,003 users. Three companies have paid licences and zero licence records. Nothing anywhere refuses to create user number n+1. In the reference, the licence count is the enforcement — it is the reason people buy more.

M10 · A successful renewal depends on a callback that may not arrive

When the nightly job charges a card successfully, it does not itself extend the plan. The new expiry date and the "active" status are written later, by the payment provider's callback. If that callback fails — and it refuses outright when it cannot resolve a company from the event — the money is taken and the plan is not extended. The next night the job runs again with a fresh safety key (the key includes the date), and charges again.

I have no evidence this has happened yet. It is a latent design fault worth closing while volumes are small.

M11 · The hourly lock-out job has no brakes

The job that deactivates users 7 days past expiry selects on the expiry date only — the plan-status filter is commented out in the source. Any company whose expiry date is stale for any reason (including the M10 callback failure) has every user set inactive and every session destroyed, every hour, whether or not they have paid. Nothing ever reverses it.

Before you can bill for usage at all — the metering project

This is the one genuinely large piece of work, and it has a natural order. Each step is useful on its own.

  1. Point the inbound rate card at a rate card that exists. Half a day. Nothing can rate inbound until this is done.
  2. One usage table per tenant. One row per chargeable event — call, message, fax — with: what it was, who, when, from, to, direction, duration or segments or pages, the rate applied, the increment, and the cost. This table is the usage history screen, the CSV export, and the evidence in a billing dispute. Build it once, properly.
  3. Write a row at the end of every call, message and fax. The call cost is already being worked out — it just goes nowhere. Send it here.
  4. Deduct from the wallet, and record the deduction. Allowance first, then balance. The bill type Balance Deduct already exists in the schema and has never been used once.
  5. Refuse to dial when the balance is empty. The single most valuable line of code in this list.
  6. Make the allowance counters real — provision them on the company at signup and increment them as usage lands. Today they are zero on all 23 companies and the usage screen honestly says it does not know.
  7. Charge for numbers. Nineteen $0.00 rental bills is nineteen months of a carrier cost we absorbed.

Then, to match the reference

  • Card verification before the first charge.
  • A real PDF invoice, with tax and fee lines itemised.
  • Enforce the licence cap when creating users, and add transfer + scheduled removal + restore.
  • The billing change log.
  • Cost centre allocations and the three CSV reports (the codes already save).
  • A written refund and cancellation policy in the product, matching whatever you decide.
  • Regulatory fees: E911 per line, universal service, relay, compliance recovery.

Leave until you need it

Licence pooling, unified credit pooling, flex-vs-contracted licences, credits analytics, spending alerts. All of these solve problems that only appear with multi-site customers or high volume. None is a launch blocker.

Part 6 — AI, chatbot and omnichannel

6.1 · The AI agent is the best-billed service we have — by a distance

Everything the reference product describes for usage billing, the AI path already does. In our own code, today:

  1. Allowance first. Each plan carries ai_call_free_minutes and ai_message_free_reply; each company gets its own copy at signup and on every renewal.
  2. A balance check before the session starts. if (freeMinutes <= 0 && rateUsd > 0 && balance < rateUsd) → allowed: false. No credit, no session. This is the exact control calling does not have.
  3. A charge at the end, in a transaction, with a row lock. Free minutes are consumed first, the remainder is priced at the plan rate, and companies.amount is decremented — all under LOCK.UPDATE, so two sessions cannot both spend the last dollar.
  4. A usage record per session. The session document carries call_billable_minutes, cost_total_usd, cost_unit_rate_usd, cost_units, cost_unit_type, call_billing_status, call_billed_at, charged_reply_ids — and a human-readable basis line such as "7 minutes @ $0.120/min".
  5. Daily roll-ups. A second collection aggregates cost, tokens, duration, sessions, sentiment and intent per company per day.

And it is genuinely running. On production right now:

AI chat sessions    45 sessions · 146 units · $0 charged (allowance covered them)
allowances moving   Capanicus  11 of 750 minutes left, 439 of 8,000 replies left
                    Testers4   49 of 50 minutes left
plan rates set      Starter $0.120/min · Professional $0.120 · Ultimate $0.030
                    Starter 50 min + 500 replies … Ultimate 750 min + 8,000

Compare that with calling: 129 AI minutes have been billed, and zero telephone minutes ever have.

6.2 · Three gaps in the AI billing, all small

  1. No ledger row. The charge decrements companies.amount and writes nothing to billings. So money leaves a customer's wallet and appears on no invoice, no statement and no usage screen. A customer who watches their balance fall cannot find out why. The bill type Balance Deduct exists in the schema for exactly this and has never been used.
  2. Chat sessions carry no billing status. Free replies are decremented one at a time and return early, so call_billing_status stays empty on all 45 chat sessions. Free versus paid is unreportable for chat.
  3. The allowance never resets on its own. It is topped back up by the renewal path. A company whose renewal fails — and four fail every night — keeps a decremented allowance indefinitely.

How to add: one Billing.create inside the same transaction as the decrement, typed Balance Deduct, with the session id in the description. Half a day, and it makes AI usage visible on the invoice, the statement and the usage screen at once — all three read the same table.

6.3 · Omnichannel is an empty shell

The route exists (/api/omni, 11 endpoints), the tables exist, the collections exist. Every one of them is empty:

omni_channels        0        omni_chat_lists   0
omni_channel_setting 0        omni_did_lists    0
                              chats             0

So there is nothing to bill, because there is no product yet. The reference product treats omnichannel sessions as a metered service drawing on the same wallet — and it is explicit that they are an older line being folded into the AI-agent product.

How to add — and the recommendation is: do not, yet. When omnichannel does ship, do not invent a third metering model. Route it through the AI agent's session-and-charge path, which already handles a session, an allowance, a rate, a balance check and a cost record. One meter, three channels.

6.4 · What this means for the metering project

The plan in Part 5 said "build a usage table, write a row per event, deduct from the wallet, refuse when empty". All four of those already exist — for AI. The work is not to design them; it is to lift the same pattern onto calling, messaging and fax, and to add the one missing piece (the ledger row) in both places at once.

That is a materially smaller and lower-risk project than this report first described, and the reviewer for it is already written: the AI path is the specification.

7.1 · The pattern underneath all three

All three add-ons are the same object: a bucket of units, attached to a licence, reset every cycle, consumed before the wallet, scoped to a set of destinations. Build that object once and all three are configuration:

bucket:  { licence_type, units, unit_type, period, scope, rollover: false }

  AI agent credits     units 750   minutes  scope: ai            (built)
  Global unlimited     units 8000  minutes  scope: 65 countries  (to build)
  Unlimited domestic   units 3000  minutes  scope: home country  (to build)
  Plan calling minutes units n     minutes  scope: plan countries(to build)

We have already built one instance of it, for AI. Generalise that rather than writing a second system.
