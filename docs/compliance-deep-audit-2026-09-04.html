Compliance — the expensive part is built, the free part is missing

4 September 2026 · a deep pass on Part B. Every claim traced to a file, a database row, or a live check on the switch.

The thesis, stated properly

The things that look expensive — a predictive dialer that obeys the FCC's abandon rule, do-not-call scrubbing on every single dial, a recorded-call announcement, our own account with the messaging registry — are already built and cost nothing to run.

The things that look free — a keyword handler for the word STOP, an upper bound on a number, a clock check before dialling, a tick-box that records consent, writing down a street address — are the ones nobody did, and each one on its own is the whole liability.

That is not an accident. Building a pacing engine is interesting work. Clamping a field to 3 is not. The interesting work got done.

1 · What is genuinely built — and it is more than I expected

1.1 · The predictive dialer obeys the FCC rule, by design

The pacing engine (campaign-api/src/services/dialer/pacing.ts, 340 lines) has three dial modes and a real feedback loop: the over-dial multiplier starts cautious at 0.8 and is pulled back whenever the abandon rate breaches the campaign's cap.

Its defaults are exactly the federal rule, which means somebody read the regulation:

targetAbandonRate:        3     // FCC: max 3% of live answers
complianceAbandonSeconds: 2     // FCC: an agent must speak within 2 seconds
maxCallsPerAgent:         3
perTickCap:               5     // a burst cannot swamp the trunk

It also carries one of only four test files in the entire 288,000-line backend — and the test is specifically "breaching the abandon cap pulls the multiplier down; staying well under lets it recover". The single most compliance-sensitive piece of code we own is also the single best-tested one.

1.2 · Do-not-call is checked before every dial, not in a nightly batch

CampaignRepository validates each candidate against the suppression list just in time, immediately before reserving it, and persists the flag so the number is skipped for good. The comment in the source says it plainly: "DNC validation is intentionally per-candidate here." That is the correct design — a list checked at upload time is worthless by the time the call is placed.

1.3 · Recorded calls are announced, and the announcement really works

The call router injects the notice on both directions — execute_on_answer=playback on outbound, pre_answer then playback on inbound. I checked the file on the live switch: 64,166 bytes, present, and FreeSWITCH's file_exists returns true. There is also an on-demand path where an agent presses *2 / *3 and the announcement is injected into the live bridge.

This is what makes recording lawful in two-party-consent states. It exists and it functions.

1.4 · Other things that are real

  • Our own account with the messaging registry. Five brands registered, all status ACTIVE with EINs on file. Most platforms rent someone else's.
  • Bot protection live on login, password reset and admin since 3 September.
  • A 72-hour purge for removed people, with an audit entry written on the purge — a deliberate, documented retention decision.
  • A security audit log with 9,565 rows, and 112 trusted-device records.
  • A per-customer concurrent-call cap, live since 3 September.

2 · Where we are exposed

Ordered by how bad it is, not by how hard it is to fix. Note the "fix" column — that is the point of this report.

E1 · Every messaging campaign we register declares that subscribers cannot opt out

In sms-api/src/repositories/TendDlcBrandRepository.ts, the payload sent to the registry is hard-coded:

subscriberOptin:   true
subscriberOptout:  false     <-- we declare people CANNOT opt out
subscriberHelp:    false     <-- and cannot ask for help
optinKeywords:     null
optoutKeywords:    null
optoutMessage:     null
helpMessage:       null

The validation schema for those fields is commented out, so nothing can override them.

Two things are wrong at once. First, supporting STOP and HELP is a baseline requirement of the messaging ecosystem — declaring otherwise is the most common single cause of campaign rejection. Second, and worse: the declaration is accurate. We genuinely do not handle opt-out (see E2). So this is not a paperwork slip; it is a truthful statement that we do not meet the standard.

Fix: about two hours — take the four keyword fields from the brand form, un-comment the schema, and stop hard-coding false.

E2 · Nothing in the platform reacts to the word STOP

I searched the whole messaging service for opt-out handling. There is none — no keyword parser, no suppression write, no confirmation reply. If a recipient texts STOP, the message is stored as an ordinary inbound message and the next campaign texts them again.

The federal revocation rule took effect on 11 April 2026 — it is in force now. It says a consumer may revoke consent in any reasonable manner, and the regulator has named the words that must work: STOP, QUIT, END, REVOKE, OPT-OUT, CANCEL, UNSUBSCRIBE. Once revoked, you have 10 business days to stop, and revocation applies to all future calls and texts to that number, not just the campaign it came from.

Statutory damages are $500 per message, $1,500 if wilful, with a private right of action — which is why this is the area that produces class actions rather than regulator letters.

Fix: about a day — a keyword matcher on inbound messages, a write to the suppression list, an automatic confirmation reply, and the same list consulted by the dialer (which it already is).

E3 · The FCC abandon cap can be switched off from the interface

The defaults are right. The limits are not:

targetAbandonRate        clamp(value, 0.1, 100)   <-- a customer may set 100%
complianceAbandonSeconds clamp(value, 0,   60)    <-- and 60 seconds instead of 2

A customer who sets the abandon rate to 100 and the window to 60 has, through supported settings on a screen we built, disabled the federal rule entirely — while the product still reports itself as compliant. The engine will faithfully pace to whatever cap it is given.

Fix: two lines. Clamp the rate to 3 and the window to 2. If a business reason ever exists to relax them, that is an internal override, not a customer field.

E4 · The abandon rate is measured per run, not per 30-day campaign

The rule measures abandons across each 30-day calling campaign. Our counters live in CampaignLiveState and are per-run — they start at zero every time a campaign is started.

So a campaign that abandons 12% of its calls, is stopped, and is started again shows a clean sheet, both to the pacing engine and to anyone reading the numbers. The engine then re-learns from scratch with the cautious 0.8 multiplier, dialling under-informed each time.

Fix: half a day — persist the counters against the campaign with a rolling 30-day window, and seed the engine from them on start.

E5 · Nothing checks the time of day before dialling

Telemarketing calls are restricted to 8am–9pm in the recipient's local time, and several states are stricter. A campaign in our platform carries a timezone field — and I found no code anywhere in the backend that compares a clock to a calling window. No calling_window, no quiet_hours, nothing.

This is the single easiest violation to commit at scale: one campaign started at the wrong hour dials every lead in the list.

Fix: half a day — derive the recipient's zone from the number's area code, refuse to dial outside the window, and hold the lead for the next window rather than discarding it.

E6 · There is no record that anyone consented

A lead has a name, a number and a status. It has no consent field — no opt-in flag, no source, no timestamp, no capture URL, no wording shown. I searched the lead model and the campaign schema; there is nothing.

Consent is an affirmative defence. In a dispute, the platform that cannot produce the artefact loses by default. This matters twice over: our customers need it, and we need to be able to show a carrier that our customers hold it.

Fix: a day — four columns on a lead (consent source, timestamp, evidence reference, wording) filled at import, and shown on the lead card.

E7 · We are not scrubbing the national do-not-call registry

Our suppression list is per company and internal — isNumberInDnc(number, companyUuid), and the whole platform holds 10 numbers.

The one federal integration we have points at api.ftc.gov/v0/dnc-complaints. That is the regulator's complaints feed — a list of numbers people have complained about. It is not the National Do Not Call Registry, which is a separate, paid subscription requiring a registered account number.

So a customer running a marketing campaign through us is scrubbing against their own list and a complaints feed, and not against the registry that actually matters.

Fix: a subscription plus about two days. This one genuinely costs money — but it is the customer's obligation, so it can be a paid feature rather than a cost we absorb.

E8 · We do not sign our calls

I searched the entire SIP proxy configuration for caller-ID authentication — no signing, no verification, no identity header handling. Our outbound calls carry whatever attestation the upstream carrier chooses to give them, which for traffic handed over unsigned is the lowest tier.

This is not theoretical housekeeping. On 3 September 2026 the regulator removed fourteen named companies from the robocall mitigation database, and every downstream provider then has two business days to stop accepting their traffic. Attestation drift from A toward C is the leading indicator your document already names as worth alerting on — and we cannot even measure it, because we do not participate.

Fix: this is a registration project, not a coding one — and only once we are a US provider. See the ordering in section 4.

E9 · Not one of our 34 numbers has a service address on file

Four tables exist for exactly this purpose. All four are empty:

identities                  0 rows
identity_address            0 rows
identity_proofs             0 rows
did_address_verifications   0 rows      (34 numbers held)

Consequences, in order of seriousness: emergency calls cannot deliver a location; the dispatchable-location requirement cannot be met; the Gate 3 control in your own fraud document has nothing to validate; and number types that the carrier gates on identity are simply unavailable to us.

The schema is built. Nobody wired the form to it.

Fix: a day for capture and validation — the postal API is free, and the tables already exist.

E10 · Five registered brands, and the data in them is junk

The registry holds five brands under our own account, all ACTIVE, all with tax IDs set. Their names are ::::::, TestConnect (three times) and Test Communications 123, all created on 1 September.

These are real registrations with a real fee against our account. And zero campaigns exist — so no messaging traffic is authorised at all today, which is the one saving grace.

Carriers audit a service provider's registry record. Five junk brands is what a poorly-run provider looks like from the outside, and your fraud document is explicit that a high rejection rate reads upstream as poor customer vetting.

Fix: an hour — delete them, and gate brand submission behind a real company name and tax ID check.

E11 · Customer-data obligations, none of which exist yet

  • Customer proprietary network information. A US provider must run a programme — access controls on call records, staff training, breach notification, and an annual certification signed by an officer, due 2 March each year. We have none of it. The one piece that exists is the recording-listen permission gate.
  • Lawful intercept capability. Nothing. This is a build-and-certify project, not a switch to flip.
  • Retention and erasure for call data. People have a documented 72-hour purge. Call history, recordings, transcripts and messages have no retention rule and no erasure path at all — so we can neither promise a customer we delete their data nor prove that we do.
  • Audit logging covers one company. 9,565 rows, all from a single tenant since 2 September. Either it is not enabled platform-wide, or only one tenant does auditable things — either way it is not evidence.
  • Nobody owns traceback. The 24-hour response deadline is a legal obligation with no named person behind it.

Add it up

Everything in the middle block — eleven items, every one of which is a real regulatory exposure — comes to roughly six developer-days and one hour of somebody's attention. Nothing in it needs a vendor, a subscription, a lawyer or a registration.

Everything in the bottom block costs real money and takes real time — and none of it applies until we are a US provider, which by the tax analysis is not yet.

4 · One piece of good luck, and what it buys you

The dialer has never really run. Thirteen campaigns exist. Agent activity records: zero. Registered messaging campaigns: zero, so no A2P traffic is authorised. Suppression list: ten numbers.

Which means every exposure in section 2 is prospective, not historic. There is no back-catalogue of violations to unwind, no consent to reconstruct for calls already made, no messages already sent to people who said stop. That is unusually lucky, and it is a wasting asset — it stops being true the first week a customer runs a real campaign.

The window to fix this costs six days and closes the moment the dialer goes live. After that, every one of these becomes both a fix and a clean-up.

5 · The order

This week — six days, no budget, no vendor

  1. Clamp the abandon cap to 3% and 2 seconds. Two lines. Do it first because it takes minutes and closes a hole that a customer can open from a screen.
  2. Delete the five junk brands and gate brand submission on a real name and tax ID.
  3. Stop declaring "no opt-out" — take the keywords and messages from the form, un-comment the schema.
  4. Build STOP / HELP handling — match the seven named words, write to the suppression list, reply once, and make it apply to all traffic to that number, not just the campaign it came from. This is the single highest-value day of work on this page.
  5. Add the calling window — recipient's local time, hold rather than discard.
  6. Persist abandon counters over a rolling 30 days, seeded on campaign start.
  7. Add consent fields to a lead and require them at import.
  8. Capture and validate the service address when a number is assigned — the postal API is free and the tables are already there.
  9. Name a traceback owner and give them an alert that reaches a phone, not an inbox.
  10. Write down a retention rule for call history, recordings and messages — and implement the deletion job that enforces it.

Alongside the metering project — Stage 1 of your own document

The spend cap and destination default-deny are unchanged from the last report, and still the biggest fraud exposure: the call router has no balance check and no destination list, so a new account can dial anywhere on earth from its first minute. Both are blocked on the same missing piece as billing — nothing debits a balance for usage. Do them in the same project.
